Who Is God?

What do we actually mean when we say God?

Our Gnan begins with reality itself. Things exist. Things happen. One thing affects another. A seed becomes a plant because certain conditions allow it to grow. A light turns on because electricity reaches it. Our bodies continue functioning through countless systems working together at every moment.

Behind what we experience, there are causes. The question is how far those causes go.

Not every cause works in the same way.

We usually think about cause and effect through time. One thing happens first, and another thing follows. Imagine a row of dominoes. You push the first one, it hits the second, the second hits the third, and the chain continues. Once you have given the first push, you can walk away. The dominoes no longer need you for the sequence to continue. The original cause started the process, transferred its power, and became unnecessary.

But there is another kind of causality. Imagine holding a domino upright between your fingers. Why is it still suspended in the air? Because your fingers are holding it. Your fingers are supported by your hand, your hand by your arm, and your arm by the rest of your body. If that support disappears, the domino falls.

This is not about what happened first. It is about what the effect depends on right now. That distinction matters when we begin thinking about God.

Follow the dependence far enough.

Most causes we encounter are themselves dependent. Your hand really is holding the domino, but your hand does not create its own ability to act. Its muscles depend on energy and biological systems. Those systems depend on other conditions. A light may be glowing because of a bulb, but the bulb depends on electricity, which depends on a larger system behind it.

These causes are real. They are simply not final. We can call them intermediate causes.

The same thing happens with authority. An employee may genuinely have the ability to make a decision because a manager has given them that authority. The manager may receive authority from someone above them. But imagine if everyone in the organization only borrowed authority from someone else, and nobody actually possessed any authority to begin with. There would be nothing to lend.

The same problem appears with causality. If every cause only receives its power from another dependent cause, we eventually have to ask where that power originates.

Our Gnan calls that final, independent Cause God.

The final Cause is Sanatan.

God cannot simply be another link in the same chain. If something else gives God His existence or power, then that deeper source would be the cause we were actually looking for. The final Cause must be independent.

This is what our Gnan means by Sanatan. Sanatan does not simply mean that God has existed for an extremely long time. It means His existence is not borrowed. He does not need something behind Him to make Him what He is. He is His own foundation.

Everything else can be traced into deeper dependence. God is where that dependence ends.

This is also why God can be described as Sarvopari, Supreme. It is not primarily a comparison of who is more powerful. It is another way of recognizing His independence: nothing stands behind God as a deeper source. And because every dependent cause ultimately receives its ability to act through that final Source, our Gnan also describes God as Sarva-Karta, the ultimate Doer of all.

God is present now.

This kind of causality also tells us something important about when God matters.

Imagine a lamp that is glowing. The fact that someone manufactured the lamp years ago does not explain why it is producing light at this moment. The light exists because power is reaching it now. Stop the current, and the light disappears.

The present effect requires present support.

In the same way, God is not simply a first event at the beginning of the universe. If reality is dependent upon a final Cause, then that dependence exists now. The causes around us may be the immediate reasons something happens, but they themselves remain dependent upon the Source behind them.

God is therefore not merely the explanation for how everything began. He is the final Cause through which anything is able to exist and act in this moment.

This is one way to begin understanding what our Gnan calls God's Pragatpanu. God is Pragat: present, active, and sustaining reality now. His connection to what exists is not something that belonged only to the past. Every present effect continues to depend upon the present Cause.

The Source must itself be actual.

There is another way to look at the same question. Imagine putting room-temperature water into a freezer.

The water has the potential to become ice, but potential alone does not make anything happen. To actually freeze the water, something has to already be cold. A freezer that only has the potential to become cold cannot freeze the water. It has to be actually operating. And the freezer itself depends on electricity, which depends on another cause behind it.

We can keep tracing the chain. Our Gnan uses this idea to make an important distinction: potential becomes actual through something that is already actual. If everything were only waiting to become something, with nothing actually possessing the power to make that change happen, nothing would ever become real.

The final Cause therefore cannot be an empty possibility. God is actual.

This also helps us understand what we mean when we say that God has form. We are not saying that God has an ordinary material body like ours. The simpler point is that actual form cannot ultimately come from something that has no actuality of its own.

The Source itself must actually be something.

Our Gnan describes God as Saday Sakar and Divya: eternally possessing His own divine form. The material forms we experience depend on causes and undergo change. God's form does not depend on something else to make it real. It belongs to the independent Source itself.

So who is God?

We began simply with the reality around us. Things exist and act through causes. Many causes are real but dependent. Those dependent causes eventually point toward a final Cause that does not borrow its existence or power from something else.

That final Cause is Sanatan: independent, eternally existing, and the ultimate Source behind every dependent cause. Because this dependence exists in the present, God is Pragat: not merely the beginning of reality, but its present support. And because what becomes actual must ultimately trace back to something actual, our Gnan understands this final Source as eternally possessing His own divine form.

This is what we mean by God.

And our Gnan identifies this God as Shriji Maharaj, Bhagwan Swaminarayan.

When we understand Maharaj in this way, we have understood more than a name. We recognize the underlying reality that the name represents: the Sanatan, independent, Pragat Cause behind existence itself.

And once we recognize who He is, the question becomes much more personal:

What does it mean that I have attained Him?
