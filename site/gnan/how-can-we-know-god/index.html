Gnan & Philosophy

How Can We Know God?

In Why Am I?, we explored how understanding the Source of our experience could lead us toward the highest experience possible. But if God is that Source, how can we, who exist within His creation, ever come to understand the Creator Himself?

We normally learn through observation, experience, reasoning, or from others. These methods allow us to understand a great deal about the world, but our senses and intellect have limits.

Our senses only show us what they are capable of sensing. Our reasoning begins with what we already know. Even when someone explains something to us, we interpret their words through our own experiences and assumptions. If God exists beyond those limits, understanding Him presents a different challenge.

So how can we know God?

We can know something truly without knowing it completely.

There's a difference between being unable to understand everything about something and being unable to understand anything about it. That distinction becomes especially important when we're talking about an infinite God.

Imagine trying to soak an entire ocean into a single strand of hair. The limitation is not in the ocean. It is in what is trying to contain it.

In the same way, Maharaj being infinite does not mean that nothing true can be known about Him. It means that whatever we understand will never exhaust everything there is to understand. We already accept this distinction in ordinary life. You can genuinely know another person without knowing every thought they have ever had. You can understand something about the universe without understanding every part of it.

Knowing does not always mean containing something completely. With Maharaj, that distinction becomes essential. We can come to know Him truly, while recognizing that His full reality exceeds the limits of our intellect.

That requires another source of knowledge beyond whatever our present understanding can generate on its own.

Some Gnan has to be received.

If there is something beyond the limits of what we currently perceive, we cannot simply invent an accurate description of it from within those same limits.

Someone who has never seen a particular place can reason about what it might be like, but there is a difference between imagining the place and hearing from someone who has actually been there. Our Gnan approaches God in a similar way. We begin by receiving an understanding from those who know through spiritual experience.

That understanding reaches us through two especially important means: scripture and Satpurush.

Our scriptures preserve Maharaj's teachings and the teachings of Enlightened Anadimuktas who spoke from spiritual experience. The Vachanamrut, Bapashri ni Vato, and other scriptures give us language through which realities beyond our ordinary experience can begin to be understood.

Scripture still has to be understood.

Words do not interpret themselves.

Two people can read the same sentence and understand it differently because they bring different assumptions, experiences, and expectations to it. The deeper or less familiar the subject becomes, the greater that problem can become. This is particularly important with spiritual Gnan. If God is being described through words that point beyond our ordinary material experience, it is very easy to interpret those words according to the reality we already know.

Our Gnan repeatedly emphasizes the importance of the Satpurush for this reason. The Satpurush does not replace scripture, and He does not become a second God. He helps us understand Maharaj from spiritual experience rather than leaving us to interpret everything only through our present perspective.

There is also something a living guide can do that a written text cannot. A scripture says the same words to every reader. The Satpurush can see the person trying to understand them. He can explain something differently, answer a misunderstanding, emphasize what matters most at a particular stage, and help someone recognize where their existing assumptions are getting in the way.

That is why the same Gnan may become clearer after years of hearing it. The words may not have changed. Our ability to understand what they were pointing toward has.

The goal is not to remain with words.

If all we ever gained were better definitions of Maharaj, something would still be missing.

Our Gnan distinguishes between knowing what something means and experiencing the reality being described. You can understand what sweetness means without tasting sugar. You can learn everything someone tells you about a place without standing there yourself. The description matters because it tells you what you are looking for, but the description is not the final experience.

Spiritual Gnan works in a similar way. Vachyarth Gnan gives us an understanding of Maharaj. It tells us who He is, how He should be understood, and what spiritual reality we are moving toward. That understanding gives our spiritual journey direction.

Then, through Satsang, the guidance of the Satpurush, and spiritual practice, what began as an understanding can gradually become lived and experienced. The Gnan that we first understood through words begins shaping how we actually see Maharaj, understand ourselves, and experience spiritual reality.

This movement from understood Gnan to experienced Gnan is Vachyarth Gnan becoming Lakshyarth Gnan. The truth itself has not changed. What changes is our relationship with it. Something that once existed primarily as an understanding gradually becomes something we recognize and experience for ourselves.

This is why our Gnan places so much importance on both Gnan and experience. Without Gnan, we may have an experience but misunderstand what we are experiencing. Without experience, we may understand the words while the reality they describe remains distant.

The two are meant to move toward one another.

Faith does not mean turning off your mind.

At some point, learning anything beyond our own experience requires trust.

We trust teachers when entering a subject we do not yet understand. We trust directions before arriving at the destination. We trust descriptions of places we have not personally visited. Spiritual learning also begins with some degree of trust.

But that trust is not meant to make questioning unnecessary. Our Gnan asks us to understand. Listen carefully. Ask what something means. Examine how the teachings fit together. Learn from the Satpurush. Then begin living and practicing what you have understood.

Trust in someone with a greater experience is meant to establish the destination. And the questions and inquiry are used to figure out a path to that destination, to their experience.

Over time, your own experience gives you more from which to understand. Something that began as another person's explanation can gradually become something you recognize for yourself.

That does not mean a finite person eventually reaches the point where there is nothing left to experience about Maharaj. If Maharaj is infinite, the experience of knowing Him can continue deepening without reaching an endpoint.

The aim is not to finish understanding God. It is to continue experience Him with more and more depth.

So how can we know God?

Not by assuming that whatever our senses immediately show us is the whole of reality. Not by believing that intellect alone can contain something infinite. And not by abandoning reason and simply memorizing the words.

Our Gnan gives us another path. We receive Gnan from Maharaj and from those who know Him through experience. We study the words that preserve that Gnan. We learn from the Satpurush who can help us understand what those words actually describe. We trust those words to be describing a greater reality beyond our perspective. We question, reflect, practice, and gradually move from an understanding of Maharaj toward an experience of Him.

We may never contain the ocean.

But we can enter it.

Once that possibility is clear, the next question becomes much more meaningful: Who is the God we are trying to know?
