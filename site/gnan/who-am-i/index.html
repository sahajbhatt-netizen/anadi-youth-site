Gnan & Philosophy

Who Am I?

It sounds like an easy question.

You could answer with your name, age, personality, career, family, culture, interests, or the roles you play. All of those things tell us something real about you. But do they fully explain the person saying “I”?

Think about how often you use that word. I'm hungry. I'm tired. I'm worried about tomorrow. I'm a student. I'm successful. I'm not good enough. I'm happy. We use the same word to describe our body, our thoughts, our emotions, our relationships, and almost everything else we experience.

Yet these things can be remarkably different from one another. Our body can be exhausted while our mind remains excited. We can feel confident one day and uncertain the next. We can be successful in one part of life and struggling in another. So which of these is actually me?

Our Gnan begins answering this question by looking at three things we commonly mistake for our deepest identity: the physical body, the mind, and the roles we play in life.

01

Am I my physical body? — Sthul Deh

If someone looked exactly like you, would they be you?

Imagine someone creates a perfect physical copy of you. Same DNA, same face, same fingerprints, same height, and every other physical detail identical. Would that person be you?

They might look exactly like you. Someone meeting both of you might struggle to tell you apart. But you would still experience life from your own perspective, and your clone would experience life from theirs. Even though the bodies were identical, there would still be two different individuals experiencing life.

It's an interesting thought experiment because we usually identify ourselves so strongly with our physical appearance. We recognize people by their faces, associate individuals with particular bodies, and even use physical characteristics to describe who someone is.

Our Gnan calls this physical body the Sthul Deh. It includes the physical attributes through which we experience and interact with the material world. Our appearance, height, physical abilities, senses, and countless other characteristics belong to this body. Much of our everyday identity also develops around it. We identify ourselves through our age, birthplace, nationality, gender, ethnicity, and other characteristics connected to our physical existence.

These things can be important parts of who we understand ourselves to be. But are they the deepest part?

The body changes, but do I?

Consider how much your body has changed. Look at a picture of yourself when you were five years old. You might barely recognize the person in the photograph. Your height, appearance, strength, and many of your physical characteristics have changed considerably. Your body will continue changing throughout your life.

Yet you still look at that photograph and say, That's me. You don't usually think of your five-year-old self as an entirely different individual who disappeared and was replaced by someone else. You recognize a continuity between the child in the photograph and the person looking at it today.

This becomes especially interesting when we consider how much of our self-worth we attach to the body. Someone might feel confident because of their appearance, athletic ability, or physical health. Another person might feel inadequate because they aren't as tall, strong, attractive, or capable as they wish to be. But these characteristics can change through age, injury, illness, or circumstances outside our control.

If our entire identity depends on something that is constantly changing, then our sense of who we are becomes vulnerable to every change it undergoes.

Our Gnan asks us to recognize that while the body is deeply important to our experience of life, it is not the entirety of the one experiencing it. We have a body. We live through it. We care for it. But the Self is something deeper than its physical characteristics.

Explore it for yourself

Pause with this

Think about how your physical identity has developed over time.

What physical characteristics do you associate most strongly with yourself?Are there things about your appearance or abilities that make you particularly confident or insecure?How would your understanding of yourself change if one of those characteristics changed significantly?Would you still recognize yourself as the same person?
Try it

Find a photograph of yourself from five or ten years ago. Spend a few minutes noticing what has changed physically, then consider the identities you associated with yourself at that age.

What felt important about your appearance or abilities back then?Do those things still matter as much today?

Notice how naturally you continue referring to both versions as I, despite everything that has changed.

02

Am I my mind? — Sukshma Deh

What if someone had your exact thoughts and memories?

Separating the Self from the physical body is one thing. But most of us would say that our identity goes much deeper than appearance. After all, even if someone looked exactly like you, they wouldn't necessarily think like you.

Your personality, memories, preferences, emotions, beliefs, and thoughts seem much more personal. They are what make you feel like yourself.

So let's return to our cloning experiment. Imagine the clone doesn't just look like you. It also has your exact memories, thoughts, personality, preferences, fears, and opinions. Everything you know, the clone knows. Every experience you remember, the clone remembers. Would the two of you now become one person? Would you suddenly begin experiencing life through both bodies?

Probably not. Even with identical minds, each of you would still have your own experience.

Our Gnan calls this mental layer the Sukshma Deh, or subtle body. Unlike the physical body, it isn't something we can directly see. It includes the inner processes through which we think, remember, interpret, feel, and respond to the world.

And just like the physical body, the mind changes.

Think about something you strongly believed as a child that you no longer believe today. Perhaps you once thought being popular was the most important thing in the world. Maybe you were terrified of something that now seems completely ordinary. Perhaps you had strong opinions about people, careers, relationships, or life itself that have changed through experience. Your understanding developed and your perspective changed, but you didn't stop being you.

Even within a single day, your mental experience can change dramatically.

You experience thoughts. You are not every thought.

Imagine receiving a message from someone saying, We need to talk. You might immediately begin worrying. Did I do something wrong? Are they upset with me? Is something bad going to happen? Your mind starts building possibilities, and before anything has actually happened, you might already feel anxious.

Then you discover that the person simply wanted to share some good news. The message didn't change, but your understanding of it did. The fear that felt so real moments earlier may disappear almost immediately.

Now consider what happened internally. You experienced a thought, believed it might be true, and felt an emotion because of it. When your understanding changed, the experience changed too.

This reveals something important: We don't simply have thoughts and emotions. We can also notice ourselves having them.

You can recognize that you're feeling angry. You can catch yourself worrying unnecessarily. You can notice that your opinion has changed or that a thought you once believed no longer makes sense. There is the thought, and there is the one aware of the thought.

This doesn't mean emotions are meaningless or that we should suppress them. Anger, sadness, fear, excitement, and countless other experiences are real parts of our lives. Some can be difficult to understand or change, even when we recognize what's happening. But they don't necessarily define the entirety of who we are.

Consider the difference between saying I am an angry person and I notice that I often become angry in certain situations. The first statement turns a recurring experience into an identity. The second gives us an opportunity to understand that experience. Why does this situation affect me so much? Where did this reaction come from? Is it something I can improve?

Recognizing the mind as something we experience creates room to understand it more clearly. Our Gnan goes further by distinguishing the Self from the Sukshma Deh itself. Thoughts, emotions, memories, and mental patterns are part of our experience, but the conscious Self is not limited to those changing mental states.

Explore it for yourself

Pause with this

Think about a strong emotion you've experienced recently, perhaps anger, anxiety, excitement, or disappointment.

What happened to bring about that emotion?What thoughts were going through your mind at the time?Did those thoughts remain the same afterward, or did your understanding of the situation change?If your thoughts and emotions can change so dramatically, should any one of them become your permanent identity?
Try it

The next time a strong emotion arises, take a moment to notice what's happening internally. Instead of immediately saying I'm angry or I'm anxious, try recognizing, I'm experiencing anger right now or I'm noticing an anxious thought. You don't need to make the feeling disappear. Simply notice the thought, the emotion, and the fact that you're aware of both. See whether that small distinction changes how you respond.

03

Am I the roles I play? — Bhumika

One person, many roles

There's another way we understand ourselves that may be even more familiar than the body or mind: the roles we play.

At home, you might be someone's son or daughter. At school, you're a student. At work, you might be an employee, manager, or business owner. Among friends, you may be the person everyone relies on for advice. Within your community, you might be a volunteer, leader, or mentor.

Our Gnan calls these roles Bhumika. Each Bhumika comes with its own responsibilities, expectations, and ways of interacting with the world.

Consider how differently you might behave depending on where you are. You probably don't speak to your manager exactly the way you speak to your closest friend. You may be playful around your siblings but serious while leading a meeting. You might be confident in one environment and uncertain in another. None of these behaviors necessarily makes you dishonest or inconsistent. Different situations naturally ask different things from us.

When a role becomes your identity

Something interesting happens when we become deeply attached to a particular role.

Imagine someone who has spent years building a successful career. Their work has become a major part of how they understand themselves. They're respected, accomplished, and proud of what they've built. Then one day, they lose their job.

Of course, losing a job can create genuine financial and practical difficulties. But sometimes the experience goes deeper than those immediate problems. Who am I now? What am I worth if I'm no longer successful? What will people think of me?

The loss of a role begins to feel like the loss of the Self.

We see similar experiences elsewhere. A student may identify so strongly with academic success that one disappointing grade feels like a personal failure. A parent may struggle when their children become independent because so much of their identity was built around being needed. Someone who has always been the responsible friend may find it difficult to admit when they need help themselves.

The roles are real, and the responsibilities attached to them matter. But problems arise when a particular role becomes the entirety of our identity.

The actor and the character

Think about an actor performing in a movie.

The actor may spend months preparing for a character. They learn the character's personality, mannerisms, background, and motivations. When filming begins, they speak, behave, and react as that character. The more convincingly they perform, the more naturally we begin seeing the character rather than the actor.

But the actor hasn't actually become the character.

When filming ends, they return to their own life. The character may have been angry, frightened, wealthy, arrogant, or deeply troubled. Those characteristics belong to the role being portrayed. The actor can portray them without making them their original identity.

Our lives aren't movies, and our responsibilities aren't fictional. But the analogy helps us understand how easily we can become absorbed in an identity.

We enter a particular Bhumika, begin looking at life through its expectations, and gradually become so accustomed to it that we forget there's an individual beyond the role. A student begins seeing everything through grades and academic success. A professional sees everything through career progression. A parent sees everything through the wellbeing and accomplishments of their children.

The role begins shaping not only what we do, but also what we value, fear, and experience. This is why identity matters so much.

The identity from which we experience something changes what that experience means to us.

A disappointing grade may feel devastating to someone whose entire identity is built around being the best student. The same grade may feel like useful feedback to someone who understands that being a student is one part of their life. The event is the same, but the identity through which it is experienced changes its significance.

Understanding Bhumika allows us to take our roles seriously without allowing them to completely define us. We can strive to become better students, parents, professionals, and friends. We can fulfill our responsibilities sincerely and appreciate the relationships those roles create. But none of them has to become the final answer to who we are.

Explore it for yourself

Pause with this

Think about the different roles you play throughout a typical week.

Which roles are most important to you?Are there situations where you feel especially confident, insecure, responsible, or pressured because of a particular role?Have you ever felt like a failure because you struggled in one role, even though other parts of your life were going well?What would change if you understood that role as something you fulfill rather than the entirety of who you are?
Try it

Choose one day to pay attention to the different Bhumika you move through. Perhaps you begin as a family member, become a student or professional, spend time with friends, and later take on another responsibility. Notice how your behavior, expectations, and emotions change between those environments. As you move from one role to another, remind yourself that you're still the same individual experiencing each of them.

The deeper Self: Atma

We've now explored three different layers of identity. The physical body changes. The mind changes. And the roles we play change depending on our circumstances and responsibilities. Yet throughout all of those changes, there remains the one having the experience.

Our Gnan identifies that conscious Self as Atma.

Atma is the eternal Self, distinct from the physical body and the subtle body. It is the conscious one who experiences, the one who knows that something is happening.

When you see something beautiful, there is the physical process of seeing, the thoughts and emotions that arise, and the one experiencing it all. When you remember something from childhood, there is the memory, the feelings connected with it, and the one aware of that memory. When you experience anger, happiness, fear, or excitement, those experiences may change considerably, but there is still the conscious Self experiencing them.

Our Gnan asks us to recognize the distinction between what is being experienced and the one experiencing it.

The body allows us to interact with the physical world. The mind allows us to think, interpret, remember, and respond. The roles we play give us responsibilities and ways of participating in society. But the Atma is not simply another one of those changing experiences. It is the Self having them.

Dehabhav: Mistaking the body for the Self

This distinction is easy to understand intellectually, but much harder to maintain in everyday life. After all, we've spent our entire lives identifying ourselves through the body and mind. We're accustomed to thinking, I am this person, with this appearance, these qualities, these problems, and these responsibilities.

Our Gnan calls this identification with the body Dehabhav.

Dehabhav isn't simply recognizing that we have a body. We obviously do, and caring for it remains important. The difficulty is mistaking that body and the identities connected with it for the entirety of who we are.

Someone insults us, and we feel that I have been diminished. Our appearance changes, and we feel that I have become less valuable. We fail at something, and instead of recognizing that an action or outcome went wrong, we conclude that I am a failure.

The changing experiences of the body, mind, and roles become the basis of our entire identity.

Atma-Gnan begins changing that understanding.

I am not merely this body, the thoughts and emotions that arise within my mind, or the roles and circumstances through which I live. I am the conscious Self experiencing them.

I am Atma.

This is the first great distinction our Gnan asks us to understand about ourselves.

Why does understanding our identity matter?

Consider how much of our everyday experience depends on what we believe about ourselves.

If I believe my value comes entirely from my appearance, then changes to that appearance can deeply affect my confidence. If I believe my worth depends on success, failure becomes frightening. If I identify completely with my thoughts, a period of anxiety or negativity can begin feeling like the whole of who I am.

The stronger the identification, the more power those experiences can have over us.

Understanding Atma offers another way to see these situations. I can experience physical discomfort without allowing it to define the entirety of my existence. I can recognize an anxious thought without accepting it as a permanent description of myself. I can make mistakes, face criticism, or struggle in a particular role without concluding that these experiences determine my deepest identity.

This doesn't mean difficult experiences suddenly become easy. Learning the definition of Atma doesn't immediately remove physical pain, emotional distress, or the responsibilities that come with life.

For most of us, Dehabhav has become deeply familiar. Even after understanding Atma intellectually, we may continue experiencing ourselves almost entirely through the body and mind.

That is why our Gnan places so much importance on reflection and spiritual practice. Through Nij-Darshan, we can begin noticing the identities, assumptions, and reactions that shape our experience. Through Dhyan, we gradually work toward experiencing the spiritual identity we have understood. What begins as an understanding can slowly become something we actually live.

Knowing who we are is only the beginning.

We've spent our entire lives learning about the world around us. We study science, history, mathematics, language, and countless other subjects. We develop skills, build relationships, and learn how to navigate the responsibilities that life brings.

But how much time have we spent understanding the one who is actually living that life?

Our Gnan begins by giving us a deeper perspective on identity. We have a physical body, a mind, and many important roles to fulfill. All of them influence our experiences, and all of them deserve attention. But none of them fully defines the conscious Self.

I am Atma.

And even this is only the beginning of the answer. Our Gnan eventually takes the identity of Atma much deeper, toward the spiritual state of Anadimukta and the Self's relationship with Maharaj.

For now, recognizing the distinction between the Self and everything it experiences gives us an important foundation.

Once we begin understanding who we really are, another question naturally follows. If I am Atma, and I've been given this extraordinary opportunity to experience human life, what is that life actually for?
