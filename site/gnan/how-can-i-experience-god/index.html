Gnan & Philosophy

How Can I Experience God?

If I have already attained Maharaj, why do I not experience Him all the time?

That question reveals an important difference between having something, understanding what we have, and actually experiencing it. Prapti gives us something extraordinary. We recognize who Maharaj is. We understand that He is Pragat and Pratyaksh. We develop Nishchay that the Maharaj before us is the Sanatan God we have been trying to understand. That recognition can bring joy, security, gratitude, and the feeling that the most important thing in life is no longer missing.

But our Gnan does not end with recognizing what we have attained. It moves toward experiencing it.

Having the food is not the same as eating it.

Guruji often explains this journey through a simple analogy. Imagine that you are hungry.

Gnan is learning what food is, recognizing what will satisfy the hunger, and understanding what you need. Prapti is realizing that the food is already in front of you. You have a full plate. You no longer have to wonder whether food exists or whether you will ever find it. It is here.

There is real joy in that recognition. If you were starving and finally saw the food in front of you, you would immediately feel relief. The uncertainty is gone. You know that what you need has been attained. But the hunger itself has not disappeared yet. For that, you have to eat.

That is where Dhyan enters the journey. Dhyan is like taking the food from the plate and actually eating it. What we understood through Gnan and recognized through Prapti begins moving inward. And Stithi is the result: the hunger is satisfied.

The food did not suddenly become more real when you ate it. You already had it. What changed was that what you possessed became part of your experience. Spiritual growth follows a similar movement.

Gnan tells us what is true. Prapti tells us what we have attained. Dhyan brings that reality into experience. Stithi is becoming established in it.

Our identity shapes what we experience.

Why is Dhyan necessary if our Prapti is already real? Because we experience life from the identity we are living from.

Most of us have spent our entire lives experiencing ourselves through the body. If someone praises the body and personality we identify with, we feel lifted. If someone rejects us, criticizes us, threatens something we value, or disrupts the life we expected to have, we feel the effect immediately.

Even after learning I am Atma, the older identity does not automatically disappear. We may understand something spiritually while continuing to experience life from somewhere else. That is the gap between Gnan and Stithi.

Suppose I firmly understand that Maharaj is the final Cause and that I have attained Him. When I remember that Prapti clearly, I may feel secure and fulfilled. But a few moments later, something happens and my entire experience becomes centered on the body again: What will happen to me? What will people think of me? What if I lose this? Why did this happen?

The Gnan did not become false. My attention and identity moved away from it.

That is why deeper spiritual experience requires more than accumulating better ideas about Maharaj. The identity from which we experience has to change.

Dhyan works on that gap.

Dhyan is not simply a way to relax the mind or concentrate more effectively. Within our Gnan, Dhyan has a deeper purpose: it helps bring the Self toward the reality we have already understood.

We know who Maharaj is. We know what we have attained. We begin understanding that the Self is distinct from the body. Dhyan gradually brings these truths out of the category of things we agree with and into the way we actually experience ourselves.

This is where the movement from Vachyarth Gnan toward Lakshyarth Gnan continues. Vachyarth gives us the meaning. It tells us what Maharaj is like, who we are, and what our relationship with Him is. Lakshyarth is when that Gnan becomes lived and experienced.

Dhyan helps close the distance between the two.

This is also why Dhyan cannot be separated from Gnan. If we do not understand who Maharaj is or who we are, concentration alone does not tell us what spiritual reality we are trying to enter. Gnan gives the direction. Dhyan works on the experience.

What does experiencing God actually feel like?

This is where language becomes limited.

We can describe the effects of spiritual experience. We can talk about bliss, fullness, fearlessness, peace, love, or complete engrossment in Maharaj. But describing an experience and giving someone that experience are two different things.

Imagine trying to explain the color red to someone who has never experienced color. You could explain wavelengths. You could point to objects that other people call red. You could compare red with every other color you know. The explanation may be completely accurate, but it still would not create the experience of seeing red.

Spiritual experience has the same limitation.

Our Satpurushas, scriptures, and kirtans sometimes use beautiful language to describe the experience of Maharaj. A kirtan may describe His Murti as “Meethi madhuri bhali murti tari murti”—sweet and deeply delightful. But that does not mean experiencing Maharaj literally tastes like honey.

The words are reaching for something that words cannot fully contain. They give us a direction. They may help us recognize something when we begin experiencing it. But Lakshyarth cannot be completely transferred from one person to another through language.

At some point, it has to become our experience.

Prapti and Stithi are different.

This is why Prapti, as extraordinary as it is, is not the end of the journey.

Prapti means the food is on the plate. I know what I have. I know who Maharaj is. I know that I have attained Him. I have the security of knowing that what I ultimately need is already here.

Stithi means that reality has become stable within my experience.

The difference can become very visible during difficult moments. With Prapti, fear may arise and then Gnan brings us back: I have Maharaj. I am His. He is the final Cause. I do not need to remain afraid.

That return is powerful. But in deeper Stithi, the spiritual identity itself becomes increasingly stable. We do not have to reconstruct it from the beginning every time circumstances change.

This is why our Gnan describes the movement from Prapti toward Stithi as a transformation of identity, not simply an increase in information. The external world can continue changing. The deeper goal is that the place from which we experience it no longer changes with it.

The deepest experience changes who “I” am.

At the beginning of this Philosophy journey, we asked: Who am I?

We began by separating the Self from the body. I am not merely this changing physical body, mind, or roles. I am Atma.

But our Gnan takes that question further. If spiritual experience depends on identity, what is the highest identity from which the Self can experience Maharaj? What happens when the separation from the body becomes complete, the connection with Maharaj becomes constant, and the Self becomes fully established in His Murti?

That is where the journey reaches Anadimukta.

The Anadimukta state describes the fullest answer our Gnan gives to both questions: Who am I? and What does it mean to fully experience God?
