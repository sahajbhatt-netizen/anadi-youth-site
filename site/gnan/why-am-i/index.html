Gnan & Philosophy

Why Am I?

In Who Am I?, we began to understand ourselves as Atma, the conscious Self experiencing life through the body and mind. But why have we been given this particular human experience, and what are we meant to do with it?

We spend much of our lives figuring out what to do. What should I study? What career should I pursue? What kind of relationships do I want? What would make my life successful, fulfilling, or meaningful?

These are important questions. But they are mostly about what we want to do with our lives. There is something deeper worth considering.

What is this life itself for? Why do I experience this and not something else? What is the opportunity I've been given, and how can I make the most of it?

Life itself is an extraordinary opportunity.

Think about what it means to be alive.

You can see the world around you, hear music, experience friendship, discover something new, and feel the satisfaction of accomplishing something difficult. You can love someone, learn from a mistake, change your perspective, and recognize beauty in things you might have overlooked before.

Perhaps most remarkably, you can reflect on the experience itself. You can ask why something makes you happy, why certain things matter to you, whether the way you're living is actually fulfilling, and what you might be capable of experiencing that you haven't yet discovered.

We rarely stop to appreciate how extraordinary that is.

Most days, we're occupied with whatever needs to happen next. We wake up, follow our routines, fulfill our responsibilities, and move from one experience to another. Life becomes something we're constantly trying to manage.

But the ability to experience life is itself something worth appreciating.

We didn't choose the circumstances of our birth, create our own bodies, or decide how long this particular life would last. Yet here we are, with an opportunity to learn, grow, choose, and discover.

Our Gnan teaches that human life is especially precious because it gives the Self an opportunity to understand its own nature and pursue the deepest spiritual experience.

So perhaps the question isn't simply how to get through life successfully. It's what we can do with this extraordinary opportunity while we have it.

What are we ultimately searching for?

Consider the things we pursue.

Someone wants a successful career because they value independence, security, achievement, or the opportunity to contribute. Someone wants a relationship because they value love and companionship. Someone travels because they want to discover something new. Someone else wants a quieter life because they value peace.

The goals themselves may be very different, but there's something they share. We're pursuing something we believe will make life worth experiencing.

Even our everyday decisions are shaped by what we value and what we hope to experience. We work toward things that seem meaningful, avoid experiences we believe will hurt us, and search for better ways to live.

But there's an interesting problem with much of what we pursue.

The happiness we experience through ordinary circumstances often depends on those circumstances remaining the way we want them. An achievement feels wonderful, but eventually we begin looking toward the next one. A vacation ends. Something we once desperately wanted becomes ordinary once we have it. Even the best moments in life are often accompanied by the knowledge that they will eventually change.

None of that makes those experiences worthless. They can be genuinely beautiful and meaningful. But it raises another question.

If we're spending our lives searching for better experiences, what is the highest experience we could actually attain?

Is there something beyond the temporary happiness that comes and goes with our circumstances? Could the Self experience a deeper joy or fulfillment that doesn't depend on everything around us going exactly as planned?

Our Gnan teaches that there is. And discovering that possibility brings us toward an entirely different understanding of what life is for.

To understand experience, explore its Source.

Think about what makes any experience possible.

When we enjoy a meal, hear a beautiful song, or spend time with someone we love, there are many things contributing to that experience. Our senses, our body, our mind, the environment around us, and the people involved all play a part.

We can explore these immediate causes to better understand why we experience things the way we do. But each of these causes also depends on something deeper.

And that raises an interesting possibility: rather than spending our entire lives searching for better experiences through the things around us, what if we explored the very Source that makes experience possible?

Our Gnan teaches that behind everything we experience is a final, independent Cause. This is what we mean by God, or Karan.

God is not simply another thing within our experience. He is the ultimate Source sustaining the reality in which any experience can happen.

And our Gnan teaches us something even more extraordinary: the deepest joy and fulfillment available to the Self comes from experiencing this Source Himself.

We identify that God as Shriji Maharaj.

This is why understanding Maharaj becomes so important. We're not exploring God simply to satisfy an intellectual curiosity about how the universe works. We're trying to understand the very Source of existence and experience, because our Gnan teaches that the highest experience we could ever attain is the experience of Maharaj Himself.

If we can begin understanding who He is, what He is like, and what our relationship with Him actually means, we can begin moving toward an experience far deeper than anything dependent on our changing circumstances.

And perhaps that is the greatest opportunity this life has given us.

A meaningful life can still look ordinary.

Discovering a deeper purpose doesn't mean abandoning the rest of life. We don't have to withdraw from the world, leave behind everyone we love, or spend our lives isolated in a cave to pursue spiritual growth.

We still have families, friends, careers, responsibilities, interests, and ambitions. These are meaningful parts of the life we've been given, and they offer countless opportunities to learn, contribute, grow, and experience.

What changes is the direction underneath them.

Rather than making our entire purpose about achieving the next milestone or finding the next source of happiness, we can begin using our lives to understand something deeper. We can learn more about ourselves as the ones experiencing life, understand Maharaj as the Source behind that experience, and gradually develop a more meaningful relationship between the two.

That understanding can change the way we approach even ordinary moments. Our relationships become opportunities to better understand ourselves and others. Our challenges can reveal the expectations and identities we've been holding onto. Our responsibilities give us opportunities to grow while remaining connected to a purpose greater than any individual achievement.

Life doesn't have to stop being enjoyable or ambitious. We can continue pursuing the things that matter to us while also recognizing that there is a much greater experience worth pursuing.

And that deeper journey can begin right where we are.

So why am I here?

Our Gnan teaches that we've been given this human life as an extraordinary opportunity to experience, understand, and grow.

As conscious beings, we spend our lives searching for experiences that bring us greater happiness, fulfillment, and meaning. But the greatest possibility available to us is not simply another experience among the many things the world has to offer.

It is to discover and experience the Source of experience itself.

That gives our lives a deeper direction: to understand who we truly are, understand Maharaj as He truly is, and develop the relationship through which we can experience life at its highest.

We began by asking who we are. Now we have a reason to explore something beyond ourselves.

If understanding the Source of experience is so important, how can we actually come to know God?
