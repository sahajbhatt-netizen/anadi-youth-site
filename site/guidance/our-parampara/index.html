Our Parampara

The spiritual life we have today did not appear all at once.

The Gnan we learn, the practices we follow, the scriptures we can open, the Mandirs and communities we gather in, and even the opportunity to learn directly from a living Satpurush are all part of a much larger story: Maharaj's Sankalp unfolding across generations.

Maharaj did not leave that Sankalp only in books or memories. Across generations, He brought extraordinary Satpurushes and Sadgurus into this work—Enlightened Anadimuktas who lived in profound spiritual experience and dedicated Their lives to carrying His vision forward. Each played a different role. Each helped bring the next generation closer to what Maharaj ultimately wanted souls to understand and experience.

That living tradition is our Parampara.

One Sankalp. Many roles.

When Shriji Maharaj manifested, His vision was never limited to creating an organization or establishing a set of rituals. He wanted souls to understand who they truly are, recognize Him as He truly is, attain the highest spiritual state, and experience His bliss.

That is an enormous Sankalp. Across generations, different parts of that vision needed to be protected, clarified, lived, preserved, and carried farther. Some established and defended the Upasna. Some revealed deeper Gnan. Some preserved that Gnan in scriptures. Some exemplified extraordinary Dhyan and discipleship. Some built communities and institutions through which the same spiritual direction could reach people across the world.

Their roles were different. The Sankalp was one.

The Parampara is more than a chain of names.

A lineage can easily become a timeline: this Satpurush came first, then this one, then the next. Dates matter. History matters. Succession matters. But none of those alone explain why the Parampara matters spiritually.

The deeper inheritance is not simply a position or title. It is Gnan, spiritual experience, principles, character, responsibility, and a commitment to carry Maharaj's Sankalp forward.

That inheritance is not always a perfectly straight organizational line either. The history includes Gurus and disciples, Satpurushes recognizing the greatness of other Satpurushes, overlapping lives, shared work, and different responsibilities unfolding at the same time.

What holds the story together is not merely who followed whom.

It is what They were all carrying.

Shriji Maharaj — the Sankalp begins.

Everything begins with Maharaj.

He revealed the Gnan, established the spiritual path, gave the Vachanamrut and Shikshapatri, formed extraordinary Sadhus and devotees, and made clear the destination He wished souls to attain.

Even before His visible Antardhyan, Maharaj was preparing for that work to continue. He brought Enlightened Anadimuktas alongside Him, entrusted Them with important responsibilities, and established a living spiritual tradition that would continue guiding seekers across generations.

But within this larger story, one manifestation holds a particularly unique place.

Jivanpran Abjibapashri — Maharaj's Sankalp Murti.

Jivanpran Abjibapashri should not be understood as simply another Sadguru in a sequence of spiritual teachers.

Our Gnan understands Him as Maharaj's Sankalp Murti—Maharaj re-manifesting for a particular spiritual purpose.

Before His Antardhyan, Maharaj had comforted devotees from Kutch who were distressed that He had not returned there for many years. He promised that although He would not return in the same visible form, His Anadimukta would manifest in Kutch, care for them, and fulfill His wishes. That Sankalp was fulfilled through the manifestation of Jivanpran Abjibapashri.

Through Bapashri, much of the Gnan that shapes Karan Satsang today became clearer and more explicit. He deeply explained Maharaj's Supreme Upasna, the state of Anadimukta, the eternal presence of Maharaj, the experience of Murti, Pratilom Dhyan, and what it actually means for a soul to move toward the highest spiritual state.

This is why so much of our modern spiritual understanding traces back to Him. The goal was not merely to know that Maharaj is Supreme, but to begin understanding what His Supremacy means. Not merely to say that Maharaj is present, but to recognize and eventually experience that presence. Not simply to speak about Moksha, but to understand the spiritual state Maharaj wishes the soul to attain.

Bapashri also ensured that this Gnan would remain available to future generations. When Sadguru Ishwarcharandasji Swami wished to preserve His deeper explanations of the Vachanamrut, Bapashri encouraged Him to begin writing, explaining that these discussions would benefit those who came later. Over many years, those teachings became the Vachanamrut Rahasyarth and Bapashri ni Vato.

Bapashri therefore holds a distinct place within this story.

The Sadgurus carried Maharaj's Sankalp across generations.

Through His Sankalp Murti, Maharaj gave that Sankalp extraordinary spiritual clarity.

The Amir Pedhi — carrying the Sankalp forward.

Alongside Bapashri's unique place is the living tradition of the Amir Pedhi: Enlightened Sadgurus who carried Maharaj's Gnan, Upasna, spiritual experience, and responsibility across generations.

Their history matters for more than the order of succession.

Each one reveals something about what it looks like to become completely devoted to Maharaj's work while remaining deeply spiritual and deeply humble.

Sadguru Gopalanand Swami — extraordinary strength, complete humility.

Sadguru Gopalanand Swami was one of Maharaj's closest Sadhus and was known for extraordinary Samarthi. Stories from His life describe spiritual power that can be difficult to understand from an ordinary perspective.

Yet His Samarthi never became a reason to make Himself the center.

After Maharaj's Antardhyan, Gopalanand Swami carried enormous spiritual and organizational responsibility across the Sampraday. He protected and spread Maharaj's Upasna, taught deeper Gnan, guided devotees, and helped preserve the direction Maharaj had established.

His life held together two qualities that might otherwise seem contradictory: immense spiritual strength and profound Daasbhav.

The greater His spiritual stature became, the less interested He seemed in ownership of it.

His greatness continually returned attention to Maharaj.

Sadguru Nirgundasji Swami — greatness recognizes greatness.

Sadguru Nirgundasji Swami continued the Amir Pedhi with tremendous Nishtha, spiritual strength, and commitment to Maharaj's Upasna.

One of the most revealing parts of His life, however, is His relationship with Jivanpran Abjibapashri.

Nirgundasji Swami was older than Bapashri and was Himself an extraordinarily accomplished Sadguru. Yet when He recognized Bapashri's spiritual identity, He did not protect His own status or try to make Himself the center. He openly directed devotees toward Bapashri and encouraged them to take His Samagam.

The Mahima moved in both directions. Bapashri spoke extraordinarily highly of Nirgundasji Swami as well. In one account, after emerging from several days of Samadhi, Bapashri received a garland and immediately offered it to Nirgundasji Swami. Nirgundasji Swami then offered it back. Their relationship reflected mutual Mahima rather than competition.

There is something beautiful in that.

Spiritual greatness does not need to defend itself from another's greatness. It recognizes it.

And when directing someone elsewhere will take them closer to Maharaj, a true Sadguru does not hesitate to point the way.

Sadguru Ishwarcharandasji Swami — preserve the Gnan so others can receive it.

Some acts of Seva become more valuable with every generation that passes.

For eighteen years, Sadguru Ishwarcharandasji Swami carefully recorded and compiled Bapashri's explanations of the Vachanamrut and His spiritual Vato. Those efforts gave later generations the Vachanamrut Rahasyarth and Bapashri ni Vato.

Think about what that means. A deeply spiritual conversation takes place at one moment in history. Someone recognizes that what is being revealed should not disappear when the people in that room are gone, so He listens, asks, writes, organizes, and preserves.

Generations later, we still learn from those moments.

Ishwarcharandasji Swami's contribution reminds us that serving a great Sankalp does not always mean being the person at the center of the story. Sometimes the essential role is making sure something invaluable survives for everyone who comes next.

Sadguru Vrundavandasji Swami — always return to the Murti.

Sadguru Vrundavandasji Swami became renowned for profound Gnan, strict Niyam-Dharma, and an extraordinary orientation toward Maharaj's Murti.

When Sadguru Muni Swami once asked Him for the easiest way to remain continuously absorbed in Maharaj's Murti, Vrundavandasji Swami could have made Himself the answer.

Instead, He directed Him toward Bapashri.

He explained that Shriji Maharaj was present in Satsang through Bapashri and encouraged Muni Swami to seek His Darshan and Samagam with eagerness.

That response reveals both spiritual clarity and humility. The purpose of the guide is not to make the seeker attached to the guide's importance. It is to help the seeker reach the deepest spiritual experience available to them.

Vrundavandasji Swami continually pointed back to that destination: Maharaj, Murti, and the inner experience of the Gnan.

Sadguru Muni Swami — a life immersed in discipleship.

Sadguru Muni Swami gives us one of the clearest examples of discipleship within the Parampara.

He lived deeply absorbed in Dhyan, travelled great distances for Bapashri's Samagam, and served Him with extraordinary personal care. His Seva was not ceremonial. He would wake Bapashri, prepare what He needed, care for His comfort during difficult journeys, and organize His own life around the opportunity to remain close to Him.

Bapashri recognized His deep inward state and gave Him the name “Muni,” reflecting His continuous absorption in Maharaj's Murti.

That spiritual inheritance would eventually be carried toward another generation through a young Devubhai—our Gurudev Bapji.

Gurudev Bapji accepted Sadguru Muni Swami as His Gnan-Guru. He remained close to Him, listened intently to His Katha-Varta, travelled with Him, and developed an extraordinary eagerness to understand the deeper principles of the Vachanamrut and Karan Satsang.

One moment between Them captures the meaning of Parampara particularly well.

What does it mean to inherit from a Guru?

In 1962, Gurudev Bapji expressed His desire to remain by Muni Swami's side and receive His inheritance.

Muni Swami initially understood the request literally. By that stage of His life, He had already distributed what little He physically possessed among the other Sadhus—including His scriptures and even the room in which He lived. He told Bapji that there was nothing material left for Him to give.

But Bapji had never been asking for material belongings.

He explained that He wanted the inheritance of Muni Swami's Gnan and principles. He wanted to become the heir of His saintliness and divine qualities.

Muni Swami became deeply pleased. He placed both hands on Bapji's head and affirmed the larger purpose for which Bapji had manifested: to carry Maharaj and Bapashri's Gnan forward and continue giving its spiritual bliss to the generations that followed.

That exchange helps explain what spiritual inheritance means in the Parampara. It is not primarily about receiving property, possessions, or even an organizational position. Gurudev Bapji wanted to receive the Gnan Muni Swami lived by, the principles He protected, the qualities He embodied, and the spiritual direction He had devoted His life to carrying forward.

That is the inheritance that matters from one generation to the next.

Gurudev Bapji — take the inheritance into the world.

Gurudev Bapji took that inheritance and gave His life to carrying it farther.

His early years were anything but glamorous. He travelled continuously with extremely limited resources, taught for long hours, lived with remarkable simplicity, endured opposition, and refused to compromise Maharaj and Bapashri's principles for comfort, acceptance, or security.

His Nishtha could be uncompromising. In His early years as a Sadhu, He travelled barefoot through villages until a devotee eventually gave Him a simple pair of four-rupee shoes. He continued using that same pair sparingly for fourteen years. When others later encouraged Him to keep money for His own security, He refused to compromise the Niyam He had accepted, expressing complete faith that Maharaj would take care of Him.

But Bapji's work was not only about what He personally renounced. It was about what He built for others.

He taught the Vachanamrut relentlessly. He propagated the Mahima of Maharaj and Bapashri. He trained Sadhus and devotees. Mandirs were established. Youth and children's activities grew. New communities formed. The Gnan moved beyond the places where it had previously been concentrated.

Eventually, SMVS became a vehicle through which this spiritual direction could reach people across India and around the world.

Bapji took a spiritual inheritance He had received personally and refused to let it remain small.

Gurudev Bapji prepared the next generation too.

A Parampara survives because someone is always thinking beyond His own visible lifetime.

Bapji did.

In 1978, He met Ghanshyambhai—the young man who would become Guruji Swamishri. Their relationship developed into one of extraordinary Gurubhakti and Atmabuddhi. Even while pursuing Chartered Accountancy, Ghanshyambhai would complete His studies late at night and still make time to sit with Bapji and listen to His Katha-Varta.

In 1980, Bapji called Him to Ghanshyamnagar and asked Him to leave that worldly direction behind and dedicate His life to Maharaj's Sankalp. Ghanshyambhai accepted, received Sant Diksha, and became Sadhu Satyasankalpdasji.

For decades, Guruji served Bapji, learned from Him, travelled with Him, helped expand His work, and continually placed His Guru's Mahima before others.

In 2012, Gurudev Bapji publicly declared Guruji Swamishri as His spiritual and organizational successor.

Once again, the person who would become the Guru had first spent His life learning how to be a disciple.

Guruji Swamishri — carrying the Sankalp into our generation.

This brings the Parampara out of history and into the present.

Guruji Swamishri carries forward centuries of Gnan, spiritual experience, sacrifice, and responsibility. But carrying the Parampara does not mean simply reproducing the past exactly as it looked.

Every generation changes. The questions people ask change. Families face different pressures. The way young people learn changes. Technology changes attention, relationships, identity, and the way people encounter spirituality.

The Gnan does not need to change.

But the way it is explained, practiced, and brought into a person's life sometimes does.

That is one of the gifts of a living Satpurush. Guruji Swamishri can take the same spiritual destination and guide this generation toward it within the reality we actually live in.

Through His Katha-Varta, Agna, Ruchi, practices, personal example, and the broader direction He gives Satsang, the Sankalp continues moving forward.

Different roles. The same spiritual depth.

Look across the Parampara and the individual personalities are striking.

Sadguru Gopalanand Swami's extraordinary Samarthi is a very different expression from Sadguru Vrundavandasji Swami's deep orientation toward Murti and spiritual absorption. Sadguru Ishwarcharandasji Swami's meticulous preservation of Bapashri's Gnan looks different again from Sadguru Muni Swami's life of Dhyan, Samagam, and deeply personal Seva.

Gurudev Bapji's fearless propagation took yet another form.

That individuality matters.

The Parampara is beautiful partly because Maharaj's Sankalp did not require every Satpurush to look identical.

Yet something deeper remains remarkably consistent across Their lives. Their spirituality was immense, but so was Their humility. They accepted enormous responsibility without making the work about personal status. They recognized greatness in Their Gurus and in one another. They served, sacrificed, and gave Their lives to something that extended far beyond Their own visible lifetime.

Their roles were different.

Their direction remained the same.

What actually gets passed forward?

Spiritual experience cannot simply be copied from one person into another. Each seeker still has to understand the Gnan, practice it, reflect on it, and gradually experience it personally.

So the Parampara does not remove the need for your own spiritual journey.

It gives that journey a path.

We inherit ways of understanding Maharaj and ourselves. We inherit scriptures that preserve the experiences and teachings of those who came before us. We inherit practices meant to move that Gnan beyond theory. We inherit examples of what profound spirituality can actually look like when lived.

And we inherit access to a living Satpurush who can help us understand all of that within the world we live in today.

We do not have to begin from zero.

We are standing on work we did not do.

Most of us did not have to protect this Gnan when it was difficult to speak openly. We did not travel from village to village to preserve and spread it. We did not spend eighteen years recording the Vato that became our scriptures. We did not endure the sacrifices required to establish the communities and Mandirs through which Karan Satsang could grow.

And most of us did not create the path through which this Gnan eventually reached North America.

We inherited the result.

That should create gratitude. But gratitude is incomplete if it only looks backward.

The Satpurushes before us did not give Their lives so that later generations could simply know Their names and admire Their stories.

They carried something forward.

Now we have received it.

The Parampara eventually reaches you.

You may never establish an organization, compile a scripture, or build a Mandir.

That is not the point.

Maharaj's Sankalp moves through ordinary life too. It moves when someone actually lives the Gnan instead of merely knowing it. It moves when a family becomes more harmonious, when a young person develops a genuine relationship with Maharaj, when someone learns to meditate rather than only speak about meditation, when a volunteer performs Seva without needing recognition, or when someone receives guidance, changes, and then becomes capable of helping someone else grow.

Your role may look nothing like the roles these Satpurushes carried.

It does not need to.

The question is whether you will recognize the part that is now in front of you.

Receive something valuable. Live it deeply. Carry it faithfully. Give it forward.

You are not outside the story.

The Parampara can feel distant when we see old photographs, unfamiliar names, and events that happened long before we were born.

But Their work is the reason so much is available to us today.

Their spiritual experiences shaped the Gnan we can now study. Their conversations became scriptures we can now read. Their sacrifices created opportunities we can now use. Their discipleship preserved a path we can now walk. And the same Sankalp continues through the Satpurush guiding us today.

So learning the Parampara should eventually leave us with one question:

What will I do with what They made possible for me?

Knowing Their history is valuable.

Allowing what They carried forward to become real in your own life is the greater response.
