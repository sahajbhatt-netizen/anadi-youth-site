Meet Guruji

You can learn someone's name, read Their biography, and know every important date in Their life without really knowing Them.

Guruji Swamishri—His Holiness Anadimukta Sadguru Shri Satyasankalpdasji Swamishri—is the Satpurush guiding our Satsang today. He manifested on October 8, 1959 in Daduka, Gujarat, later studied Commerce and Chartered Accountancy, and received Sant Diksha from Gurudev Bapji in 1980. In 2012, Gurudev Bapji publicly declared Him as His spiritual and organizational successor.

Those facts tell you something about His life. But to begin understanding who Guruji is, you have to watch how He lives.

He usually asks of Himself first.

When Guruji wants people to develop a practice, He rarely stands at a distance and simply tells everyone else what to do. He begins.

When He encouraged people to spend less time absorbed in their phones and develop greater focus, devotion, and peace through Mala, He took up the practice Himself. Even with an intense schedule of Sabha, travel, meetings, personal guidance, and responsibility for a global Satsang, He maintained His own demanding standard while giving others room to begin gradually.

That pattern appears throughout His life. Before asking, “Why aren't people doing this?”, He seems much more interested in asking, “Am I showing them what this looks like?” It is one of the simplest ways to learn from Guruji: pay attention to what He asks of Himself before looking at what He asks of everyone else.

He can correct you without defining you by your mistakes.

People sometimes come before Guruji carrying things they are ashamed of: mistakes, habits, decisions, guilt, or parts of their past they wish they could change. He may not pretend that nothing happened. If something needs to improve, He will say so. But correction is not the same as condemnation.

After listening, guiding, and sometimes firmly challenging someone, Guruji has repeatedly encouraged people to stop carrying the identity of a mistake after it has been confessed and addressed. He does not want someone spending the rest of their life staring backward at a version of themselves they are trying to leave behind.

One story captures this beautifully. Two young devotees once gave Guruji lengthy letters describing mistakes from their past. After reading them, Guruji later performed Dandvat before them. Confused, they asked how He could treat them that way after knowing everything they had written. His response was simple: once it had been confessed and forgiven, He had left it behind, and they should too.

That combination is easy to miss. Improve, but do not let the past become your permanent identity. Be accountable, but keep moving forward. Guruji expects growth because He seems determined to keep seeing what a person can become.

He thinks like a sculptor.

Guruji often shares a story about a painter working on a mural. The painter kept going over the same small area again and again. Eventually, Guruji asked why he was spending so much time on one spot when an entire mural remained unfinished.

The painter explained that he could not move on until that part matched the vision in his mind. Guruji saw something of His own work in that answer.

People sometimes wonder how He can repeat the same teachings for years with the same urgency. Why speak again about the same weaknesses? Why return to the same practices? Why keep working on something that seems to be improving so slowly? Because He has not stopped seeing the person they can become.

Guruji describes His work almost like that artist: patiently shaping, correcting, encouraging, and returning to the same place until the person in front of Him moves closer to their spiritual potential. That is also why some of His strongest messages can feel remarkably practical. Harmony within a family, speaking kindly, forgiving quickly, living with discipline, taking care of the mind, developing better habits, learning to see Maharaj, and growing spiritually may look like separate topics, but for Guruji they are parts of the same person being shaped.

His idea of being a Guru looks a lot like serving.

Guru Purnima is normally a day when everyone turns their attention toward the Guru. Guruji has found ways to turn that attention back toward others.

On one Guru Purnima, He quietly arranged to recognize people whose Seva could easily remain invisible: young Sadhus, volunteers working behind the scenes, and people serving in parking, cleaning, dishes, and other roles that rarely place someone on a stage. He brought them forward, honored them, and performed Dandvat before them.

This is not an isolated idea in His life. Even after correcting someone firmly, Guruji has been known to return, apologize if He worries He was too harsh, and make sure the person still feels cared for. The person occupying the role of Guru seems remarkably uninterested in protecting the status of being one.

When Gurudev Bapji became Antardhyan, Guruji described the responsibility left to Him not as a throne He had inherited, but as a Seva Gurudev had entrusted to Him. That difference tells you a great deal about Him.

His simplicity shows up in very ordinary places.

Some of the most revealing Guruji stories are not dramatic spiritual events. They are about a car, an old audio player, or a pillow.

When people wanted Him to replace a car that still worked, He saw little reason to do so. When an old audio player stopped playing properly, He tested it carefully and discovered that only the USB needed replacing. A new device was unnecessary.

During travel, attendants once tried to quietly bring His usual pillow from center to center so He could remain comfortable. Guruji noticed. Eventually, He marked the pillow, caught them carrying it again, and asked them to stop. Whatever was available at the next center was enough.

These are tiny decisions, but that is precisely why they matter. A person's principles are easier to admire in exceptional moments. Character becomes more revealing when no exceptional moment is required. Guruji's simplicity is visible in what He does not seem to need.

He notices people.

There can be thousands of people around Guruji, and somehow the individual can still enter His attention. During a Sabha, He may notice an elderly person getting up and make sure someone helps them walk safely. During a large Darshan line, He may notice that one child received Prasadi while their sibling did not and call the child back.

These are small moments, but think about what has to happen internally for someone to notice them. Your attention has to keep leaving yourself.

This may be one of the easiest parts of Guruji's life to imitate immediately. You do not need His responsibilities, His schedule, or His role. You can simply begin noticing people.

At the center of everything is Maharaj.

It would be easy to encounter Guruji only as a leader. He oversees an organization, travels, teaches, meets people, and carries enormous responsibility. But underneath that visible life is something much deeper.

Earlier in His life, Guruji would spend many hours each day in Dhyan. When Gurudev Bapji asked Him to turn outward and use His life to help others experience that spiritual bliss, Guruji changed His routine and took on greater responsibility.

Yet the inner life did not disappear. Guruji still sets aside extended periods of Ekant. When asked what He does in that solitude, He describes not loneliness but continuous companionship with Maharaj and the bliss of His divine presence.

Even after returning late from travel or spending long hours helping people, Guruji protects His morning Seva of Harikrishna Maharaj. He personally wakes Maharaj, performs His Seva, and prepares Him. For Guruji, this is not another task in the schedule. It is love.

The organization, the travel, the teaching, the projects, and the people make more sense when you see what sits underneath them: Maharaj is at the center.

The Guru is still a disciple.

Perhaps one of the most useful things to learn about Guruji is how He speaks about His own Guru. Despite being the Satpurush whom others now look toward for guidance, His relationship with Gurudev Bapji remains deeply that of a disciple.

Once, during a discussion about Gurubhakti, someone asked whether He missed Gurudev. Guruji paused. His eyes filled with emotion as He explained how deeply He missed Him. One of the things He missed was surprisingly specific: who would now do His rok-tok?

Who would stop Him? Who would correct Him? Who would challenge Him when He needed it? Think about the attitude underneath that question. Many of us tolerate correction because we know we should. Guruji misses the person who corrected Him.

The person teaching us to become teachable remains an extraordinary example of teachability Himself. The person showing us how to become disciples still lives as one.

What seems to matter to Him?

Spend enough time listening to Guruji and patterns begin to appear. He wants people to experience Maharaj more deeply, not merely know more religious information. He wants spirituality to enter ordinary life. He wants families to live with greater harmony. He wants people to stop carrying unnecessary guilt and begin growing. He values discipline without harshness, simplicity without show, Seva without status, and a relationship with Maharaj that becomes increasingly real.

Most of all, He seems unwilling to settle for spirituality remaining theoretical. Gnan should change the way you think. Practice should change what you experience. And your relationship with Maharaj should eventually change the way you live.

That is the direction He continues to push toward.

Do not stop at “Guruji is amazing.”

Try it

It is easy to hear one of these stories and admire Him. Admiration is a good beginning, but it is not the end.

Take the story about the child who did not receive Prasadi. The lesson is not merely that Guruji has remarkable attention. Ask what allowed Him to notice. Perhaps it reveals care, presence, or a habit of looking outward rather than constantly remaining absorbed in Himself.

Then personalize it: Who do I routinely fail to notice?

Now Guruji's story has moved from being about Guruji to changing something within you. That is how Mahima begins becoming discipleship.

Get to know Him for yourself.

No page can fully introduce a person, especially a Satpurush whose spiritual experience goes far beyond what can be captured in a short biography.

Listen to Guruji's Katha-Varta. Watch how He speaks to people. Pay attention during Darshan. Learn the Prasangs shared by those who have spent time with Him. Notice His repeated Agna and Ruchi. Ask what His decisions reveal about what He values.

When one Prasang stays with you, do not rush past it. Sit with it. Try to understand what you are seeing, then take one small part of it into your own life.

The more closely you learn to observe Guruji, the less He remains simply someone you know about. You begin learning from Him.

And eventually, you may begin understanding why so many people struggle to explain their relationship with Him using facts alone. Sometimes the simplest description is enough: you have to experience it for yourself.
