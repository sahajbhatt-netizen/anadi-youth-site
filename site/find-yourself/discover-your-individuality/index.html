Discover Your Individuality

You did not become who you are on your own.

Your family, friends, culture, school, Satsang, work, experiences, and the people you follow have all shaped you. They influence what feels normal, what you admire, what you avoid, and what you imagine a good life should look like.

That is not a problem. We learn through other people.

But there is a difference between being influenced and never noticing the influence at all. Individuality begins with learning to see that difference more clearly.

This is not about becoming different for the sake of being different. Rebellion can be just as reactive as conformity. The point is to understand yourself well enough to make more conscious choices.

01

Social media & people you admire

The people we follow can introduce us to new ideas, interests, habits, styles, careers, and ways of living. That can be useful. But repeated exposure also has a way of making unfamiliar things begin to feel desirable.

A certain lifestyle starts looking normal. A product suddenly feels necessary. Someone else's routine becomes the standard you compare yours against. Sometimes the influence lasts. Sometimes it disappears as soon as you stop seeing it.

Learning to notice that difference can tell you a lot about what is actually taking root in you.

Pause with this

Who are three people or accounts you pay the most attention to?

What do you find yourself admiring about them?

Have you started wanting, buying, doing, or valuing anything partly because you saw it through them?

Which of those things still feel important when you have not been consuming their content for a while?

Try it

For one week, notice when a new desire appears while scrolling or shortly afterward.

Make a quick note of what you saw and what it made you want. At the end of the week, look back and see which desires stayed with you and which faded quickly.

You do not have to judge either one. Just notice the pattern.

02

Approval & fitting in

Most of us adjust ourselves depending on who we are around. You probably do not speak to your closest friend the same way you speak to your boss or your grandparents.

Sometimes that adjustment is simply good judgment. Other times, we start hiding parts of ourselves because fitting in feels safer than standing apart.

It may be something small: pretending to enjoy something, keeping quiet about an opinion, or going along with a plan you did not really want. Those moments are easy to dismiss, but over time they can show us where other people's approval has more influence than we realized.

Pause with this

Start with the people you spend the most time around: family, friends, coworkers, classmates, Satsang friends.

Around whom do you feel the least need to monitor yourself?

Around whom are you more careful about what you say, enjoy, wear, or admit?

Can you remember a recent moment when you went along with something mainly because everyone else did?

What did you think might happen if you chose differently?

Were you protecting belonging, approval, respect, your image, or something else?

Do not worry about deciding whether your response was right or wrong yet. First understand what was happening.

Try it

Choose one small, low-risk situation this week where you would normally follow the group automatically.

It might be choosing what to eat, what to watch, sharing an opinion, admitting that you like something, or saying you would rather do something else.

Pause before answering. Make the choice intentionally, and then notice what happens inside you.

03

Family, culture & what you inherited

Some of the strongest influences in our lives arrive long before we are old enough to examine them.

We grow up learning what counts as respectful, successful, embarrassing, responsible, spiritual, practical, or normal. Families and cultures can pass down enormous amounts of wisdom. They can also pass down assumptions that made sense in one context but may deserve another look in ours.

The point is not to question something simply because it was inherited. It is to understand what we are carrying and why.

Sometimes that reflection confirms that a value is deeply worth keeping. Sometimes it reveals that we have been following an expectation without ever really making it our own.

Pause with this

Growing up, what did the people around you consider a “good life”?

What kinds of careers, relationships, achievements, behaviors, or lifestyles were praised?

Which of those values do you now understand and genuinely want to carry forward?

Is there anything you still feel expected to want, even though you are not sure why?

Try it

Pick one expectation you grew up with and write it as a simple sentence:

A successful person should…A good son or daughter should…By this age, I should…People like me are supposed to…

Then ask yourself whether you would still choose it if nobody expected it from you, and why.

You do not have to change the expectation afterward. The exercise is simply to understand your own reasoning.

04

Comparison & your idea of success

Comparison often reveals what we value before we have put those values into words.

We tend to compare ourselves with people who seem to have something that matters to us: money, confidence, attention, intelligence, a relationship, physical appearance, discipline, influence, spirituality, or a particular kind of life.

That reaction can be useful information. Sometimes another person shows us something we genuinely want to develop. At other times, the comparison exposes a definition of success that we have absorbed without thinking much about it.

Instead of treating comparison only as something to stop, we can sometimes use it as a clue.

Pause with this

Who have you compared yourself with recently?

What did they have that made the comparison matter?

What did you imagine having that thing would change about your life or about how you see yourself?

Would you still want it if nobody else ever knew you had achieved it?

Try it

The next time you notice yourself comparing, complete this sentence:

I wish I had their ______ because I think it would give me ______.

Try to fill in both blanks without overthinking it.

Then leave it there for a while. The second blank may tell you more than the first.

You are not trying to become uninfluenced.

There is no version of you that developed separately from everyone else, nor would that necessarily be desirable.

Individuality is being able to learn from someone without needing to become them. To take advice seriously without handing over every decision. To belong somewhere without requiring yourself to match everyone around you.

The more clearly you see your influences, the more deliberately you can decide what deserves to remain part of your life.

You probably will not figure all of that out from one page. That is where Nij-Darshan becomes useful. The situations above are simply places to start noticing. Over time, ordinary experiences can keep showing you what shapes you, what matters to you, and why.
