How to read diacritics

Seeing a word like Swāmī, charaṇ, or kīdhī for the first time can make the little marks above and below the letters look more complicated than they really are.

They are simply pronunciation guides.

When Gujarati, Hindi, or Sanskrit words are written using the English alphabet, several different sounds can end up looking identical. Diacritics help preserve those differences so you can get closer to the original pronunciation even if you cannot read the original script.

You do not need to memorize everything before using the Prayers & Kirtans library. Learn a few common marks, listen to the audio when it is available, and the rest will become familiar naturally.

Start with the vowels

The line above a vowel usually tells you to hold that sound a little longer.

ā — an “aa” sound as in father; example: Swāmī.

ī — an “ee” sound as in see; example: kīdhī.

ū — an “oo” sound as in food; example: chhūṭī.

ṛ — a short “ri” sound, found more often in Sanskrit words.

The difference can be small when you first hear it. You do not need to exaggerate the long vowel. Simply give it a little more space.

For example, Swāmī is closer to Swaa-mee than Swa-mi.

Dots under letters change where the sound is made.

You may also see letters such as ṭ, ḍ, ṇ, ṣ, or ḷ. These represent sounds made with the tongue slightly farther back in the mouth than their regular English-looking versions.

ṭ — a “t” pronounced with the tongue curled slightly back.

ḍ — a “d” pronounced with the tongue curled slightly back.

ṇ — an “n” pronounced with the tongue curled slightly back.

ḷ — a Gujarati “l” pronounced with the tongue farther back in the mouth.

ṣ — a deeper “sh” pronounced with the tongue slightly farther back.

You do not need perfect tongue placement to understand or sing a kirtan. The marks mainly tell you that the sound is not quite the same as an ordinary English t, d, n, l, or s.

That is why charaṇ ends differently from the English word ran, even though both would otherwise end in the letters “ran.”

A few other sounds you may see

ś is pronounced like sh, as in shine.

ṅ sounds like the ng in sing.

ñ is close to the ny sound in canyon.

A dot or nasal mark associated with a vowel can indicate that the sound should be lightly nasalized. Rather than making a completely separate “m” or “n” sound, some of the sound passes through the nose.

What about letters followed by h?

In words such as kh, gh, th, dh, ph, or bh, the h usually indicates a small release of breath after the consonant.

So th in an Indian-language transliteration usually does not mean the English th in think or this. It is closer to a t followed by a small puff of air.

The same idea applies to dh, bh, and similar combinations.

Do I need to pronounce everything perfectly?

No.

Diacritics are there to help, not to turn prayer into a pronunciation test.

Read the transliteration as closely as you can, listen to the accompanying recording when one is available, and gradually let your ear do more of the work. After hearing the same words repeatedly, many of the distinctions that initially look technical begin to feel natural.

More importantly, do not let unfamiliar spelling become a barrier to understanding what you are praying.

The words matter because of what they are helping you express.

A quick example

Take a line from the Swaminarayan Aarti:

Charaṇ saroj tamārā, vandu kar joḍī

You can read it in pieces:

Charaṇ

The dot beneath ṇ tells you that the final “n” is pronounced with the tongue slightly farther back.

tamārā

The lines above the ā vowels tell you to give those “aa” sounds a little more length.

joḍī

The dot beneath ḍ changes the “d” sound, while ī gives the final vowel an “ee” sound.

You do not have to stop and analyze each mark every time you sing. The guide simply helps you understand what those marks are telling you until the pronunciation becomes familiar.

When in doubt, listen.

Transliteration can get you close. Hearing the words sung or spoken gives you something even better.

Where audio is available in the Prayers & Kirtans library, use the two together: read the words, listen to the pronunciation, understand the meaning, and then make the prayer your own.
