Jay Sadguru Swāmī

Swaminarayan Aarti

Aarti can become so familiar that it is easy to sing every word without thinking about what we are actually saying. But Jay Sadguru Swāmī is filled with Mahima. It speaks about who Maharaj is, what it means to take His refuge, the happiness He gives, His compassion, His eternal presence, and the extraordinary opportunity of having attained Him.

Understanding those words can turn Aarti from something we complete into something we experience.

The story behind the Aarti

The Aarti was composed by Muktanand Swami during the early years of Shriji Maharaj's work. After Ramanand Swami passed away, Sahajanand Swami began revealing more of His divine power. People were experiencing samadhi through Him, and reports of what was happening eventually reached Muktanand Swami while he was in Bhuj.

Muktanand Swami was concerned. What he was hearing seemed different from what he had known before, so he returned to understand what was happening for himself. When he met Maharaj, however, he was met not with pride but extraordinary humility. Maharaj welcomed him warmly, offered him His own seat, and bowed before him.

Muktanand Swami's initial anger began to settle, but he was still trying to understand what he was witnessing. That night, the question continued to trouble him. The account records that Ramanand Swami appeared to him and firmly reminded him of Sahajanand Swami's supreme identity.

Muktanand Swami realized his mistake. He returned to Maharaj with a garland and prepared a flame for Aarti. Out of that moment of realization, surrender, and Mahima came the words we continue to sing today: Jay Sadguru Swāmī.

What are we actually saying?

Jay Sadguru Swāmī

“Jay Sadguru Swāmī, Prabhu Jay Sadguru Swāmī. Sahajānand dayāḷu, baḷvant bahunāmī.”

The Aarti begins with praise. We offer reverence to Shriji Maharaj and remember who He is: compassionate, immensely powerful, and known through many divine names.

That beginning matters. Before asking Maharaj for anything, the Aarti first shifts our attention toward Him. For a few moments, the center is no longer our plans, worries, responsibilities, or problems. It is Maharaj.

I take refuge at Your feet.

“Charaṇ saroj tamārā, vandu kar joḍī. Charaṇe shīsh dharyāthī, dukh nākhyā toḍī.”

With folded hands, we bow at Maharaj's lotus feet. This is more than a physical gesture. Placing our head at His feet represents refuge and surrender. The things we have been trying to carry entirely by ourselves are placed before someone greater than us.

The verse expresses the security that comes from having Maharaj as that refuge. Whatever may be happening around us, we have attained someone beyond it. Aarti can therefore become a real moment of surrender, a chance to recognize what we are carrying and consciously place some of it at Maharaj's feet.

Maharaj is the giver of true happiness.

“Swāminārāyan sukhadātā, dvijkuḷ tanu dhārī. Pāmar patit udhāryā, agaṇit nar nārī.”

Maharaj is described as the giver of happiness. He appeared before people in a human-like form, but the Aarti asks us not to stop at that outward appearance. His nature is divine, and He came for the liberation and spiritual upliftment of countless people.

The next line emphasizes His compassion. Maharaj did not come only for people who were already spiritually accomplished. He uplifted those who were struggling, imperfect, and in need of Him. That matters personally because it means we do not have to become perfect before approaching Maharaj. We approach Him because we are not.

His divine work continues.

“Nitya nitya nautam līlā, kartā avināshī. Aḍasaṭh tīrth charaṇe, koṭi Gayā Kāshī.”

Maharaj is Avinashi, imperishable and eternal. His Leela is described as ever new. The verse asks us to recognize Him not merely as someone belonging to history, but as eternally divine.

It then expresses the immense Mahima of His feet. The spiritual value traditionally associated with countless sacred pilgrimages is described as already present at Maharaj's feet. The deeper point is Prapti: what are we searching for elsewhere that we have already attained in Maharaj?

Aarti becomes an opportunity to remember the value of what is already in front of us.

He is Pragat.

“Purushottam pragaṭ nuṁ, je darshan karshe. Kāḷ karma thī chhūṭī, kuṭumb sahit tarshe.”

This verse moves directly into one of the deepest ideas in our Upasna: Purushottam is Pragat. Maharaj is not only a God who existed sometime in the past. Our Gnan teaches us to recognize Him as present and accessible.

Darshan therefore becomes more than looking at the Murti. It is learning to recognize the divine Maharaj before us with the understanding of who He truly is. The verse connects that realization with liberation from the forces that otherwise bind a person, including Kal and Karma.

When this verse comes during Aarti, it is worth asking quietly: Who am I looking at?

That question can completely change Darshan.

Recognize the opportunity you have received.

“Ā avasar karuṇānidhi, karuṇā bahu kīdhī. Muktānand kahe mukti, sugam karī siddhi.”

The Aarti ends with gratitude. Maharaj, the ocean of compassion, has shown extraordinary grace. Muktanand Swami recognizes the magnitude of the opportunity before him and declares that Maharaj has made the path to liberation accessible.

There is a beautiful progression through the entire Aarti. We begin by recognizing Maharaj, then take refuge at His feet, remember His compassion and divinity, recognize Him as Pragat, and finally realize how much we have received.

That progression is Mahima.

What is Aarti helping us cultivate?

Aarti can help cultivate Mahima, surrender, gratitude, refuge, and awareness of Maharaj's presence. The flame, singing, and physical ritual give us something to do, but the deeper practice is internal. For those few minutes, we are training ourselves to move our attention away from ourselves and toward Maharaj.

Knowing the meaning gives each line direction. When you sing “Charaṇe shīsh dharyāthī,” you can surrender something. When you sing “Swāminārāyan sukhadātā,” you can remember where lasting happiness ultimately comes from. When you sing “Purushottam pragaṭ nuṁ,” you can look at the Murti and remember who is before you. When you sing “Ā avasar,” you can allow yourself to feel grateful for what you have attained.

The words stop being only words and begin giving direction to the mind.

Try Aarti differently.

Try it

The next time you participate in Aarti, do not try to think deeply about every line at once. Choose one line before Aarti begins and decide to pay attention to it.

Understand what it means, and when that line arrives, slow down internally and let yourself actually pray it. On another day, choose a different line.

Over time, the words may stop passing by automatically. They begin becoming thoughts you recognize, feelings you understand, and eventually something you mean.

That is when Aarti becomes more than something you sing every day. It becomes a way of returning to Maharaj.
